I wrote a post about [failure resilience](/blog/entry-level-proxmox-failure-resilience/) with a tidy little table in it.
Tier 1 was for DNS and NTP: mirrored boot drives, ECC memory, replicated storage, HA enabled, automatic failover in seconds.
I meant every word, and every word of it worked.

Then a storm took my power out for thirty-one hours and showed me the table was missing a column.

## What Actually Happened

The utility failed just after noon.
I run two groups of gear on two different UPSes, and until that afternoon I had never thought carefully about the difference between them.

One UPS carries the servers.
It was holding about 366 watts, it predicted twelve to fourteen minutes of runtime, and it delivered fourteen minutes and five seconds.
An honest UPS.

The other carries the modems, the gateway, the switching, and the access points — under 200 watts — and it ran for hours.

So the shape of the outage was this: **the network stayed up and the computers went dark.**

Which sounds survivable, and would have been, except for one thing.
All three of my DNS resolvers are containers on the Proxmox cluster.
They went down together, fourteen minutes in.

That produced the strangest failure mode I've had at home.
Phones and laptops were on their own batteries.
The WiFi was up.
Both WAN links were up.
And nothing resolved — not my own hostnames, not anything.
A working internet connection that nobody could use.

## Two Tiers, and Only One of Them Matters at 2 a.m.

Sketching it out afterward, the two UPS groups turned out to deserve names:

**Subsistence Internet** — the minimum required to stay connected.
Modems, gateway, one PoE switch, a couple of access points.

**Luxury Internet** — everything else.
The cluster and its guests: file service, cameras, monitoring, the DVR, and, as it turns out, DNS.

The names are the useful part.
Once you have them, the placement question asks itself: during an outage, is this service subsistence or luxury?
DNS is obviously subsistence.
It had been living in the luxury tier for years.

## Redundancy Only Protects You Along the Axis You Spread It On

Here's the part I got wrong, and I got it wrong *carefully*.

The three resolvers are deliberately spread one per cluster node, so no single node failure can take down more than one of them.
Each is an HA resource, each replicates its disk to the others, each can restart elsewhere in seconds.
Against node failure, that is genuinely good engineering.

Against power, it is worth nothing at all.
All three nodes sit on the same UPS.

<CalloutBox type="warn" title="Count your failure domains, not your copies">
	Spreading N copies across N nodes protects you from losing a node.
	It does nothing about anything all N nodes share — a UPS, a breaker, an uplink, a rack, a room.
	Redundancy is only redundancy along the axis you spread it on.
	Three copies in one failure domain is one copy with extra steps.
</CalloutBox>

## And a Spare Nobody Is Told About Is Not a Spare

There is a second, dumber half to this.

DHCP was handing out exactly one resolver.
So even if one of the three had somehow survived, no client would have thought to ask it.
My name service was one deep on the wire no matter how many copies were running.

That was the actual root cause, and fixing it was one edit to one field.

## The Fix Is a Raspberry Pi

Not another cluster node.
A bare Pi running BIND as both a caching recursive resolver and an authoritative secondary of the primary — full root recursion, no forwarder, nothing upstream to have an opinion about my queries.
The zone copies persist to disk and the SOA expire is 28 days, so it keeps answering authoritatively for about four weeks after the primary disappears.
That is longer than any outage I intend to have.

Three choices in there were deliberate:

- **Same software as the others.** BIND, with the same configuration idioms, because a survivor you administer differently is a survivor you will misconfigure.
- **Bare metal, not a container.** The entire point is to not be on the cluster.
- **Not highly available.** Making the survivor HA would put it right back on the tier I'm trying to escape.

It had a previous life as a GNSS reference station on a full desktop image, so most of the work was stripping it back down: no desktop, no print service, no mDNS, no RPC, until the only things listening were DNS and SSH.
A survivor should be small.
Every service it runs is another way for it to not be there when you need it.

And DHCP now hands out all four resolvers, with the Pi last.
Clients prefer the cluster and fall through to the survivor only when the cluster is gone.
The cost is a few resolver timeouts during a total outage, which is exactly the right trade.

## The Subsistence Tier Is a Watt Budget

Here's what surprised me once I started drawing the two tiers as a picture.

The subsistence tier is not "the important gear."
It is "the gear that fits in the watt budget."
Every watt you add is minutes you take away, and the arithmetic is unforgiving: runtime scales with how small you can make the load, which is why the same class of battery gave me fourteen minutes on one tier and hours on the other.

My access points make the point nicely.

| | Older APs | Newer APs |
|---|---|---|
| Model | U6 Lite | U7 Pro XG |
| Max power | 12 W | 22 W |
| Power method | plain PoE | PoE+ |
| Uplink | 1 GbE | 10 GbE |

The newer ones are genuinely better.
They are faster when you are near one, and they earn their place on mains power.
They also draw nearly twice as much, demand more from the switch, and run hot enough that you notice when you touch one.
Wonderful habits. Expensive ones on battery.

So the older access points are the subsistence pair.
They give coverage everywhere at twelve watts apiece, and the fast hot ones are allowed to go dark.
On the subsistence tier, *good enough everywhere* beats *excellent nearby*.

## The Same Mistake, Three More Times

Once you have the phrase "shared fate," you start finding it everywhere.
Each of these is the same error as the DNS one, wearing a different hat:

- **The monitoring was a guest of a machine it monitors.**
  It died fourteen minutes in — so my entire outage-alerting budget is fourteen minutes, and the notification path itself probably depended on gear that was also dying.
- **Both UPS network cards lost their wall clocks.**
  Their own event logs are now stamped in 2010 and are useless for dating the outage, so I had to reconstruct the timeline from the monitoring system that died at fourteen minutes.
  The instrument that records a power failure should not be a casualty of it.
- **Fourteen minutes of runtime were available and none of it was spent shutting anything down.**
  No low-battery threshold was set, and nothing was wired to act on one if it had been.
  The nodes were not shut down; they were cut off.
  That is the most actionable finding in the whole post-mortem, and it has nothing to do with batteries.

## Three Questions Worth Asking About Your Own Setup

1. **Which UPS feeds which machine?**
   I still cannot fully answer this, which is embarrassing to write down.
   Tracing outlets to hosts is now on the list, and until it's done, no post-mortem I write can say what *should* have stayed up.
2. **Does anything you'd want during an outage live only on the tier that dies first?**
   Name resolution, certainly. Think about what else — a password manager that syncs, a door lock controller, the thing that tells you the power is out.
3. **Are clients actually told about the survivor?**
   A survivor nobody queries is decoration.

## What's Still Undone

The Pi resolver is built, verified, and being handed out by DHCP — but it is not yet plugged into the subsistence UPS, which is the entire point of it.
A PoE switch arrives tomorrow to make that island physically self-contained, down to the switch port the Pi lands on.
A wrong switch is as easy to get wrong as a wrong UPS, so both are getting labeled.

And one thing I cannot resolve from my desk.
Both UPS cards report 1000 VA and 990 W, and I am fairly sure I bought 1500 VA units.
If the cards carry the wrong nameplate, every load percentage and runtime estimate above is suspect with them.
The one number that survives either way is the fourteen minutes, because that came from a log that simply stopped.

So the next step is to go read a physical label — which is a fitting end for a post about the difference between what you configured and what you actually have.
