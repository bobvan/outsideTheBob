I set out to research the limits of clock sync precision — effectively chasing the
last nanoseconds to UTC.

**Is sub-nanosecond UTC sync possible? Literally, no. Practically, yes.**

That sounds like a dodge, and it isn't. The gap between those two answers is the
most useful thing I learned, and it turns on a distinction that's easy to skate
past: **agreeing with another clock is not the same as being right about UTC.**

I gave this talk at the [London](https://docs.stacresearch.com/fall2025LON) and
[New York](https://docs.stacresearch.com/fall2025NYC) STAC Summits, to audiences
who care about this for financial markets.

## Why You Literally Cannot Get Below About 5 ns

Two things stand in the way, and neither is about your equipment.

**The constellations themselves sit about 5 ns from UTC.** Even the best GNSS
constellations have a standard deviation around 5 ns relative to UTC. That isn't
a rumour — it's published. Look for `sigma_GPS` in a recent
[Circular-T from BIPM](https://webtai.bipm.org/ftp/pub/tai/Circular-T/cirt/cirt.454)
and check me on it.

**UTC doesn't exist in real time.** A national lab like NIST has atomic clocks
that do far better than 5 ns, but
[UTC is a virtual timescale](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/time-realization/utcnist-time-scale-0/introduction-utcnist),
computed after the fact from contributions around the world.

The mechanism is worth spelling out, because it's the part that surprises people.
Each country's time lab watches the satellites, compares what comes down against
its own earthbound clocks, and reports the differences to the BIPM in Paris. Then
— weeks to months later — the BIPM publishes what UTC *was*.

There is no wire you can plug into that carries UTC as it happens. As I put it in
the talk: everybody else on Earth is *predicting* UTC. The BIPM is *defining* it,
in arrears.

So if you're getting time from GNSS rather than from a national lab, you cannot
literally be closer than about 5 ns to UTC. That ceiling is structural, and no
equipment budget moves it.

## Why You Can Practically Do Far Better

Now the interesting part.

In my homelab, two high-quality GNSS receiver modules —
[u-blox F9T](https://github.com/bobvan/f9tResearch) — agreed with each other to
**550 picoseconds** over half a day, while each wandered 6 to 7 nanoseconds
against GPS.

Two conditions made that number possible, and I skipped past both in the talk for
lack of time. You need to track a **single constellation**, because the
constellations don't agree with each other to better than about 5 ns. And you need
a **dual-frequency** receiver, so the ionosphere can be measured out rather than
modelled. Take either away and the result doesn't hold.

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Agreement is not accuracy">
	If a constellation is running 5 ns ahead of UTC, every receiver tracking it is
	5 ns ahead of UTC too — together. The shared error cancels between them.
	Two clocks can agree sub-nanosecond while both are wrong about UTC by an order
	of magnitude more than that.
</CalloutBox>

Whether that's good news depends entirely on your question.

If you need to prove to a regulator that a timestamp is within some tolerance of
UTC, the 5 ns floor is your problem and no amount of care in the lab removes it.

But if what you actually need is for two machines to agree on the order of events
— which is what most people in markets are really asking — then the constellation's
offset from UTC is a common-mode error that cancels. You care about the 550 ps,
not the 5 ns.

Most of the confusion I've seen in this area comes from conflating those two
questions.

## The Part That Bites in a Datacenter

All of the above assumes you can put an antenna where it needs to be. In a
datacenter you usually can't, and this is where the theory meets a roof.

The roof blocks the sky, so the antenna has to go on top of it — either yours, or
one the facility provides. Either way you are likely to be missing the two
quantities you most need: **the length of the feed line**, and **an accurate
position for the antenna**.

That matters because of where time is and isn't corrected for delay. Inside the
distribution network, NTP and PTP subtract out the delay they measure, so time
looks the same everywhere. But the antenna feed line sits *outside* that
compensated region, along with the pulse-per-second signal. Nothing measures those
for you. If you want time that is honestly traceable to what arrived at the
antenna, you have to know the feed line's delay and subtract it yourself.

A cable of unknown length is a constant, unknown offset — which is precisely the
kind of error that a beautiful clock cannot fix.

## What I Haven't Tested Yet

This is the state of GNSS clock sync as I see it in late 2025. Two developments
look like they could move the floor and I haven't put either on the bench:

- [Galileo HAS](https://www.gsc-europa.eu/galileo/services/galileo-high-accuracy-service-has),
  which may bring their standard deviation down.
- [Fugro AtomiChron](https://www.fugro.com/expertise/satellite-positioning/atomichron),
  which promises sub-nanosecond sync to their own traceable reference timescale.
