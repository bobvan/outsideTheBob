A GNSS receiver looks like it is reading time out of the sky. It is not.

Each constellation runs its own timescale — GPS Time, Galileo System Time — and
each of those is steered to track UTC as closely as its operator can manage. What
your receiver recovers is that timescale, plus a broadcast offset telling it how
far the operator believes their timescale currently sits from UTC.

Which makes GPS Time, in the phrase that stuck with me:

> A real-time prediction of what USNO will eventually define as UTC(USNO).

Prediction, because [UTC has not been computed
yet](/timekeeping/utc/what-utc-is/). Real-time, because you need an answer now.
And *eventually*, because the definitive one arrives about a month later in
Circular T.

## The satellites are free-running, and drifting

The clocks aboard the satellites are excellent and they are not steered
continuously. They run free and drift, and the ground segment tracks that drift
and broadcasts corrections for it — where the satellite really is, and what its
clock really reads, against the prediction.

So the signal you receive is a free-running clock plus a correction computed on
Earth. **Everything that separates a hundred-nanosecond consumer fix from a
sub-nanosecond disciplined clock lives in those corrections**, not in the raw
observations, which are what they are regardless of what you spend.

<CalloutBox type="warn" title="Corrections have a shelf life">
	This is the part that makes the prediction framing concrete rather than
	philosophical. Precise correction streams are re-issued <strong>every five
	seconds</strong>, and a stream that hands you a five-second-old correction is
	meaningfully worse than its interval suggests.

	Something that needed refreshing that often was never a measurement of the
	present. It was a forecast, and forecasts decay.
</CalloutBox>

The ladder of correction sources runs from the coarse broadcast navigation
message, through free signal-in-space services like Galileo HAS, to
subscription streams from individual analysis centers — each fresher or more
accurate than the last, and each still a prediction.

## Why you cannot get below a few nanoseconds *to UTC*

Everything above compounds into a floor. Between the constellation's own
prediction error, the broadcast offset's granularity, and the delay before UTC is
defined at all, aligning to **UTC** in real time runs out of road at a few
nanoseconds.

But that is a statement about UTC, not about clocks.

## When you can just call it GPS Time

If your requirement is that your clocks agree with *each other*, and you do not
need to hand a timestamp to somebody outside your estate, then the constellation's
timescale is a perfectly good reference — and you can align to **GPS Time or
Galileo System Time specifically, far below the floor that applies to UTC.**

Everyone tracking the same constellation shares its prediction error. It is
[common-mode](/timekeeping/glossary/#common-mode), and it cancels in every
comparison you make internally.

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="This resolves an apparent contradiction on this site">
	The <a href="/timekeeping/transferring/matching-acquisition-and-distribution/">matching
	table</a> shows sub-nanosecond as achievable, and this page says you cannot get
	below a few nanoseconds. Both are true, because they are about different
	things.

	Sub-nanosecond <em>agreement</em> with a constellation timescale: achievable.
	Sub-nanosecond <em>trueness</em> against UTC: not, in real time, by anyone.
</CalloutBox>

So the useful question is not "how close to UTC can I get?" but "does anything I
do actually require UTC?" For a great deal of datacenter work the answer is no,
and knowing that is worth a lot of money.
