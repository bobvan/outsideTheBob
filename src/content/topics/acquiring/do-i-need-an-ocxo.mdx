Every GNSS-disciplined clock is two clocks in a trench coat, and they have
opposite personalities.

**The oscillator is a flywheel.** Intrinsically quiet over short intervals —
there is a high-Q resonator in there and nothing to average — but it drifts and
ages over long ones, because it has no idea what time it is.

**GNSS is a noisy absolute reference.** Poor over short intervals, because it is
a statistical estimate from finite-strength radio signals through a turbulent
atmosphere. Excellent over long ones, because it is anchored to the UTC ensemble
and you can average as long as you like.

Plot both against averaging interval and **they cross**. Everything on this page
follows from where.

## The crossover decides which clock you actually have

The discipline loop is tuned to that crossing, and what comes out is the lower
envelope of the two curves:

- **Below the crossover** — short intervals — the loop cannot correct that fast,
  so the output *is* your oscillator. The flywheel shows through.
- **Above the crossover** — long intervals — the loop has steered the oscillator
  onto GNSS and averaged the noise away. The output *is* GNSS.

The whole game is to place that crossing well, so you get the oscillator's
short-term quiet **and** GNSS's long-term truth.

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Which answers the question directly">
	Your oscillator only matters <strong>below the crossover.</strong> Above it,
	you could swap in something far worse and see no difference at all, because
	the loop is correcting faster than the oscillator can misbehave.

	So "do I need an OCXO?" is really: <em>does my application care about
	intervals shorter than the crossover?</em> If everything you do is measured
	over minutes, the answer may genuinely be no.
</CalloutBox>

## An upgrade buys two separable things, and people conflate them

This is the part that saves money, because most oscillator upgrades are sold on
the wrong half.

**Improving short-term noise** — the region where the oscillator is the limiting
curve — lowers your output noise there *and* pulls the crossover inward, because
a quieter oscillator now beats GNSS out to a shorter interval. You can see this
in normal running.

**Improving long-term drift and aging** changes **nothing** in normal locked
operation. GNSS discipline is already masking it. It buys you exactly one thing:
[holdover](/timekeeping/acquiring/what-happens-when-i-lose-gps/) — how long you
coast when the antenna goes deaf.

Both are worth money. They are worth money for different reasons, and a vendor
quoting aging figures at you is answering the holdover question whether or not
that was the one you asked.

<CalloutBox type="warn" title="The degenerate case, and it is common">
	An oscillator so poor that GNSS beats it even at the shortest intervals pushes
	the crossover to zero. There is no region where the flywheel helps.

	At that point the oscillator matters <strong>only</strong> for holdover — and
	you are, in normal running, simply reading GNSS with extra steps.
</CalloutBox>

## The entry ticket, in numbers

The useful figure of merit is [ADEV](/timekeeping/glossary/#adev) at one second —
the fractional frequency instability over a one-second average.

| class | ADEV at 1 s | what it means here |
|---|---|---|
| TCXO | ~1 × 10⁻¹⁰ | free-running noise above a 1 Hz loop consumes a whole nanosecond-class budget by itself |
| entry OCXO | ~1 × 10⁻¹¹ | the entry ticket for nanosecond work |
| good OCXO | ~5 × 10⁻¹² | comfortable |
| double-oven OCXO | ~1.5–2 × 10⁻¹² | the top of what a small module does |

That first row is the answer for most readers. **A TCXO does not fail
gracefully at nanosecond scale — it fails by consuming the entire budget before
anything else gets a turn.** If your requirement has a nanosecond in it, about
10⁻¹¹ is where the conversation starts.

If your requirement has a *microsecond* in it — which covers a great deal of
datacenter work, and all of
[NTP-class distribution](/timekeeping/transferring/distribution/) — a TCXO is
genuinely fine and an OCXO is money you could have spent on the
[antenna](/timekeeping/antennas/antenna-position/) instead.

## What the parts actually cost

From a lab survey done in April 2026, shopping for four units under $100 each.
Prices are used, from the usual auction sites; ADEV at 1 s is from datasheets
where published and family estimates otherwise.

| part | ADEV at 1 s | phase noise at 10 Hz | used price |
|---|---|---|---|
| IsoTemp OCXO-131-100 | ≤ 2 × 10⁻¹¹ | ~−115 dBc/Hz | $20–25 |
| NDK ENE3311B | ~1 × 10⁻¹¹ | ~−130 dBc/Hz | $3–6 |
| CTI OSC5A2B02 | ~5 × 10⁻¹² | ~−125 dBc/Hz | $3–7 |
| Morion MV89A | 2 × 10⁻¹² | −130 dBc/Hz | $30–60 |
| Rakon STP3593LF *(in the SparkPNT GNSSDO+)* | 1.5 × 10⁻¹² typ | −130 dBc/Hz typ | not sold separately |

Two things worth taking from that table.

**The entry ticket is a few dollars.** A part an order of magnitude better than
the TCXO in your receiver costs less than lunch on the used market. The reason
to hesitate is never the oscillator's price — it is the board, the oven's power
draw, the warm-up time, and the fact that a 51 × 51 mm can does not fit anything.

**The good ones are big and hungry.** The two best parts on that list are
double-oven SC-cut designs in packages too large for the common carrier boards,
and one of them wants 12 V. Physical fit and power kill more oscillator upgrades
than performance does.

## What actually decides it in practice

Having said all that: on a real installation the limit is usually not the part
number.

It is **temperature**. An oscillator's frequency follows its own temperature,
and in holdover that dominates everything else — a thermal transient can be
worth [a factor of several](/timekeeping/acquiring/what-happens-when-i-lose-gps/)
against the same crystal in a quiet room. An oven attacks exactly that term,
which is why an OCXO beats a TCXO in holdover by far more than the ratio of
their quoted stability figures suggests. You are not buying a better crystal so
much as buying independence from the room.

And it is **the receiver**, in a way that surprises people. A noisy PPS forces a
low loop bandwidth, which pushes the crossover out to hundreds of seconds and
strands you on the oscillator alone across the whole middle of the range. In
that situation a better *receiver* improves your clock more than a better
oscillator would, because it lets you close the loop faster. Which is the
argument the
[receiver traits page](/timekeeping/acquiring/what-makes-an-accurate-timing-receiver/)
makes from the other side.

## How to decide, in one pass

1. **What is the shortest interval you care about?** If it is longer than your
   crossover, stop — your oscillator is already irrelevant to normal running and
   you are shopping for holdover.
2. **Does your budget have a nanosecond in it?** Then about 10⁻¹¹ at one second
   is the floor, and a TCXO is not a candidate.
3. **How long must you survive without GNSS, and to what threshold?** That is the
   [holdover](/timekeeping/acquiring/what-happens-when-i-lose-gps/) question, and
   it is the one aging figures actually answer.
4. **Is the room stable?** If not, fix that first — it is cheaper than the
   oscillator and it moves the number further.
5. **Check the package and the supply before falling in love.** The best part in
   your price range probably does not fit.

A rubidium is the next rung and it is a different kind of purchase — its
short-term noise is not necessarily better than a good OCXO's, and what you are
buying is the long end: drift small enough that holdover stops being measured in
minutes. If your answer to question 3 was "hours, to a tight threshold", that is
the conversation. If it was not, a rubidium is an expensive way to solve a
problem you do not have.
