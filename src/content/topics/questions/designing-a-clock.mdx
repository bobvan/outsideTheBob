Building your own is where the vocabulary stops being vocabulary. Every term in
the [glossary](/timekeeping/glossary/) turns into a decision, and the decisions
divide sharply into two kinds: errors you can correct later, and errors you are
choosing to live with permanently.

That division is the through-line of this page.

## 1. Which of your errors are compensable?

Some paths have their delay measured and subtracted — that is what NTP and PTP
do across a network. Others do not: the pulse-per-second line and the antenna
feed line carry delay that nothing corrects for but you. Knowing which region a
wire is in decides whether a mistake there is a nuisance or a permanent offset.

## 2. What are you steering to, and what is *it* steering to?

You cannot steer to [UTC](/timekeeping/glossary/#utc), because it does not exist
yet. You steer to a realization — GNSS, a lab's
[UTC(k)](/timekeeping/glossary/#utc-k), a house standard — and inherit its
error. Decide deliberately which one, and know what its published offset history
looks like.

## 3. What is your oscillator's stability at the interval your loop cares about?

Not its headline figure — its [stability](/timekeeping/glossary/#stability) at
the averaging interval where your control loop actually lives. Below that
interval the oscillator is carrying you alone; above it the reference is. The
crossover is a design parameter, not a datasheet number.

## 4. How often do you observe, and how often do you actuate?

These are separate decisions and conflating them is a common way to build a
worse clock than the parts deserve. Observing more often buys information;
actuating more often injects your own noise into the output. The interval that
is right for one is rarely right for the other.

## 5. What resolution does your timestamping need?

Your [resolution](/timekeeping/glossary/#resolution) has to be finer than the
precision you are trying to achieve, or you cannot see your own performance —
and worse, you will appear to have achieved it. Decide the number you need
before choosing the counter, not after.

## 6. Where is your uncompensated path, physically?

Trace it: antenna, feed line, connectors, PPS cable, and everything between. Its
length is a fixed offset in your output, and the arithmetic is unforgiving —
[a wrong velocity factor on a 30 m run is tens of nanoseconds](/timekeeping/antennas/feed-line/).
This is the error most likely to survive every other thing you get right.

## 7. How will you know your position, and how will you know it is right?

If you are disciplining from GNSS, your antenna's surveyed position is an input
to the time solution, and
[a receiver's own survey is biased](/timekeeping/antennas/antenna-position/).
Decide now whether you will accept that bias, post-process a better answer, or
have the position surveyed — because it is not something averaging will fix
after the fact.

## 8. What will you do when the reference disappears?

[Holdover](/timekeeping/glossary/#holdover) is not a feature you add later; it
is the oscillator you already chose, plus whatever you learned about it while
the reference was present. Decide what error threshold matters and for how long,
and check whether your oscillator can honor it.

## 9. How will somebody benchmark this?

Design for measurability: bring out a clean PPS, document the delays you have
compensated and the ones you have not, and say what reference you steered to. A
clock that cannot be
[independently measured](/timekeeping/questions/benchmarking-clocks/) is a clock
nobody can trust, including you.
