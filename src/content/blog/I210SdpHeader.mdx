The retail Intel Ethernet Server Adapter I210-T1 has a six-pin header on it, 2×3
on a tenth-inch pitch. If you want a network card that can timestamp an external
pulse per second — which is what you want if you are disciplining a clock to
GNSS — this is one of the few cheap ways in, and that header is how you reach it.

Two people have written down what is on it. Neither finished.

Richard Cochran's [i210-rework
page](https://linuxptp.sourceforge.net/i210-rework/i210-rework.html), which is
where everybody starts, gives pin 2 as SDP0, pin 3 as ground and pin 4 as SDP1.
Pins 1, 5 and 6 are marked `?`. jclark's
[pc-ptp-ntp-guide](https://github.com/jclark/pc-ptp-ntp-guide/blob/main/pps-nic.md)
carries a TODO: *"Top left is probably GND. Bottom right might be SDP2. Bottom
left is probably not GND."*

Those are the two best sources that exist for this card, and they stop in the
same place. So I put it on the bench.

## The answer

Numbering across the short axis first, then down, with the RJ45 to the left:

| pin | position | signal | how I know |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | top-left | **GND** | continuity to the bracket |
| 2 | top-right | **SDP0** | PPS applied, timestamped by SDP0 |
| 3 | mid-left | **GND** | continuity to the bracket |
| 4 | mid-right | **SDP1** | PPS applied, timestamped by SDP1 |
| 5 | bottom-left | **not an SDP** | 1 kΩ to ground; a PPS on it is invisible to all four SDPs |
| 6 | bottom-right | **`NVM_SI`** | traced to pin 5 of the flash chip |

That numbering is the same one Cochran uses — his table independently has pin 2
as SDP0, pin 3 as ground and pin 4 as SDP1 — so if you find his page and this one,
the pin numbers mean the same thing on both.

If you are holding a card and want to be sure which corner is pin 1, find that
resistor. It sits a couple of millimeters from contact 5 and nothing else on the
header has a discrete component next to it, so once you have it the other five
follow. The two right-hand contacts in the upper rows have clean traces heading
off toward the controller, which is SDP0 and SDP1; the two left-hand ones have no
surface trace at all, because they drop straight into the ground plane.

So the card gives you **two usable SDPs, not four**. And the guess that the
bottom-right pin might be SDP2 is wrong in a more interesting way than being
merely incorrect: pins 5 and 6 are not SDPs at all. They are a jumper, and
somebody put them on that header on purpose.

## Two of the pins belong to the flash chip

Pin 6 goes to pin 5 of a Winbond W25Q16JVNIQ, a 2 MB SPI flash sitting on the
card. On that package pin 5 is DI — the flash's data *input*.

Here is the part that makes the identification awkward to read. Intel names the
i210's NVM pins from the **flash's** point of view, not the controller's, so an
`_SI` pin is an output:

```
NVM_SI    pin 12    T/s   Output   Connect this lead to the input of the Flash.
NVM_SO    pin 14    T/s   Input    Connect this lead to the output of the Flash.
```

Header pin 6 is i210 pin 12, `NVM_SI`, an output driving the flash's data input.

Table 2-1 of the datasheet lists `NVM_SI` as having an external pull-down or
pull-up, with a footnote: *"When pulled up, Flash security features are
enabled."* That makes `NVM_SI` the `SECURITY_EN` strap, and pin 5's kilohm to
ground is its provisioned pull-down. Shorting the two pins together pulls the
strap up and turns NVM security off.

Section 3.4.3 says what that is for, in so many words:

> By disabling the `SECURITY_EN` strapping option, the iNVM lines left blank
> become writable again… useful for fixing iNVM values that were programmed
> wrongly, or if boards are resold to a third party who wants to further
> customize the iNVM. For example, the third party might want a different MAC
> address or device ID.

Which explains the header. It is not an accident of routing and it is not a
debug leftover. Two of the six pins are a factory-provisioning jumper that
happens to share a connector with the timing pins.

<CalloutBox type="warn" title="This is the pin that bricks cards">
	Three things before anybody reaches for a jumper. It is a <em>reset-time</em>
	strap, so it has to be in place before the card powers up — fitting it to a
	running machine does nothing. Intel's own text says security must be
	re-enabled after an iNVM write cycle. And security off plus a bad write is
	exactly how cards end up needing an external SPI programmer to recover. I am
	documenting what the pins are, not recommending you short them.
</CalloutBox>

## How I worked out the rest, and the control that made it mean anything

The method is the transferable part, so it is worth more space than the pinout.

**Drive outputs at different frequencies so they identify themselves.** Arming
two periodic outputs at 1 Hz and 4 Hz rather than both at 1 Hz means a scope on a
pin tells you *which* channel reached it, not merely that something is happening.
That distinction paid for itself immediately, because it exposed a driver
behaviour I would otherwise have spent a long evening on: I armed two channels on
SDP2 and SDP3, and their frequencies came out of the SDP0 and SDP1 pins.

The reason is in `igb`. `igb_pin_perout()` sets the routing bit for the pin you
asked for and never clears it for any other pin, and the disable path does not
clear it either. Routing is cumulative. Muxing a channel onto a different pin
**adds** an output rather than moving it. The only code that releases a pin is
the external-timestamp path, which is why releasing one properly means muxing it
to input, enabling, disabling, and dropping it back to none.

**Then test the other direction, because a pin that will not drive may still
listen.** Apply a PPS to one header pin at a time and see which SDP timestamps
it.

And here is the part I would want somebody else to take from this:

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="The first four runs returned zero, and none of them were results">
	Four pins tested, four times nothing detected — and not one of those nulls was
	worth anything, because the detector had never detected anything at all. A
	broken detector and a real null are the same reading. They only became results
	after a positive control on a pin I knew was good returned 24 events in 12
	seconds with a pulse width of 5.000 ms and no spread.
</CalloutBox>

That control carried a second check I had not planned. The card's clock was slow
by 30.3 ppm by one measurement and 29.7 ppm by another — the first from
differencing it against the system clock hours earlier, the second from the
period this run reported, 0.999970 s against a true second. Two unrelated routes
to the same quartz, agreeing to better than a part per million. At that point the
nulls meant something.

The same run gave me a negative control for free: with the pulse on pin 4, SDP1
reported it and SDP0 reported nothing. No crosstalk, no leftover routing on the
input side.

## Knowing when to stop

Pin 6 I never tested electrically, and the reason is the most useful thing on
this page for anyone probing unknown hardware.

It measured **60 Ω one way and −2 Ω with the leads reversed**.

A resistor reads the same in both directions. Asymmetry means a junction or a
driven node, and a negative reading means current is coming *out* of the pin into
the meter. Driving 2.5 V into 60 Ω would be 42 mA, against a pin rated for six.
So I stopped and traced it on the board with a magnifier instead, which is how it
got identified as the flash line at all.

If I do go back to it, the safe version is 3.3 V through 10 kΩ — a third of a
milliamp no matter what is on the other end. Too weak to overcome the kilohm
pull-down on pin 5, plenty for a high-impedance input.

## What this means if you have one of these cards

**Two SDPs, so three signals will not fit.** A clock that has to emit a pulse per
second, emit a second signal, and accept a pulse per second from a GNSS receiver
needs three pins and this card has two. That is a hardware limit, not a
configuration you have not found yet.

**Both edges get timestamped.** The v2 external-timestamp ioctl is a dead end on
this driver — it rejects every flag beyond the enable, so asking for a single
edge fails and asking for no edge fails. The v1 call works and gives you both
edges. Budget two events per pulse and compute the period from every *other*
event. Take consecutive gaps instead and they alternate between the pulse width
and the period minus the pulse width, so the listener reports 0.994970 s and you
appear to have a 5000 ppm clock error. I did exactly this on the first run. The
arithmetic gives it away: 0.994970 + 0.005000 = 0.999970, which is the period.

**Four SDPs are a property of the controller, not of this card.** Intel's own
reference design brings all four out to an eight-pin header. A custom board could
have them. But SDP2 and SDP3 have never been confirmed working on an i210 in my
lab — they were armed, the driver accepted the request without complaint, and
nothing came out anywhere I could reach. On this card that is fully explained by
those pins not going to the header. From outside, though, "not routed here" and
"does not work" are the same observation, and I would not design a board that
assumes four good SDPs on the strength of it. Settling that costs one sacrificial
card and Cochran's rework.

## If you add your own pins

The stock header is a shrouded block you cannot easily get a probe or a jumper
into, so sooner or later you will want pins on it, and there is a second
footprint beside it that looks like an invitation.

<CalloutBox type="warn" title="Those pads lift, and they take the connection with them">
	The pads next to the header are <strong>surface mount, not plated
	through-holes</strong>, and they are not on tenth-inch centers either — so a
	standard header does not drop into them and you end up holding a part at an
	angle while you solder it. A through-hole can take some abuse because the
	plating runs through the board. A surface pad is a foil of copper held on by
	adhesive, and a little torque in the wrong direction peels it straight off.
	<strong>I wounded one i210 doing exactly this.</strong>

	What worked much better on the next card was not a header at all:
	<strong>strain-relieved SMA pigtails soldered to the pads</strong>, with the
	cable anchored so that nothing a hand does at the connector end reaches the
	joint. The coax takes the load, the pad never sees it, and the signal comes
	off the card on something you can actually plug a scope or a PPS into.
</CalloutBox>
