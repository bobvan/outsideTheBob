## B. Technical corrections, ranked

### B1. The TCXO-ceiling claim contradicts PePPAR-Fix's central thesis

`acquiring/what-makes-an-accurate-timing-receiver.mdx`, trait 6 callout:

> if the receiver will not accept your oscillator as its own timebase, its
> measurements are made against its TCXO, and **you cannot build a clock with
> better short-term stability than that TCXO out of them.**

That is not right, and it is the one paragraph in the garden that a reader
who knows the field will use to dismiss the rest. A disciplined OCXO with a
low loop bandwidth *routinely* has better short-term stability than the
receiver's TCXO — the loop simply does not act up there. It is the moonshot
target as written in PePPAR-Fix's own `CLAUDE.md`:

> at every tau, the DO output is as stable as the *best* of (DO free-running
> noise floor, RX TCXO noise floor). At short tau the better oscillator's
> noise floor should shine through unmolested by the discipline loop.

The true statement is about **measurement and correction**, not output:

> Every servo input you derive from that receiver is timed against its TCXO,
> so inside your loop bandwidth you inherit the TCXO's noise, and above it you
> are flying blind — your oscillator may well be quieter than the instrument
> watching it, and you have no way to prove it or to correct it.

The parallel callout on `can-i-build-my-own-link-to-utc-nist.mdx` ("Which
puts a ceiling on the whole exercise") is **fine as written**, because that
page really is about the measurement.

### B2. "A foot of extra antenna cable is a nanosecond" — it is about 1.5 ns

`datacenters/how-big-is-a-nanosecond.mdx`. The 30 cm/ns rule is a *free
space* rule. The moment it touches coax it needs the velocity factor, and
your own feedline page says so with numbers: 5.05 ns/m at VF 0.66 → **1.54 ns
per foot**; 3.92 ns/m at VF 0.85 → 1.20 ns per foot. Two heavily cross-linked
pages currently disagree by 50%.

Suggested: *"A foot of extra antenna cable is about a nanosecond and a half —
light does a foot per nanosecond, but coax does about two thirds of that, which
is [a page of its own](/timekeeping/antennas/feedline/)."* That turns an error
into a hook for the page you most want read.

The neighbouring claims are correct: "a GNSS antenna surveyed a meter too high
is three nanoseconds" ✓, "30 ps ≈ 9 mm" ✓, "1 ps ≈ 0.3 mm" ✓.

### B3. UTCr is not a prediction

`utc/can-i-sync-datacenter-clocks-to-utc.mdx`:

> **UTCr**, weekly since 2013, is BIPM's *rapid* UTC — explicitly published as
> a prediction, narrowing the wait without closing it.

UTCr is a rapid **post-hoc** computation, not a forecast: weekly, daily
values, covering a period already past. Your own
`how-gnss-time-relates-to-utc.mdx` describes it correctly ("published weekly
since July 2013 with daily values… agrees with *Circular T* to within a few
nanoseconds"), so the two pages contradict each other on a page whose whole
argument is prediction-versus-definition. Fix the `can-i-sync` sentence; the
distinction it needs is *rapid but still retrospective, and still not the
reference for traceability* — which is exactly the callout you already wrote
on the other page.

### B4. The AtomiChron power-budget inference has an obvious counterexample

`utc/choosing-a-gnss-timescale.mdx`:

> **Ambiguity resolution is not a cheap computation.** … not what a passively
> cooled sub-watt module with an RF chain to feed looks like it is doing.

A ZED-F9P resolves integer ambiguities — decorrelate, search the lattice,
validate — continuously at a few hundred milliwatts, on hardware you have on
the bench. Integer AR is exactly what sub-watt modules do all day, and a
reader who does RTK will know that in one second. The inference does not
survive it.

The rest of the callout does survive, and is the better argument anyway: an
L-band feed at a couple of kbit/s is sized for orbits, clocks and code biases,
not for **per-satellite phase biases**, and without phase biases from a single
self-consistent analysis center nothing can be fixed regardless of compute.
Cut the compute half, keep the link-budget half, keep the "it is labelled a
hunch" honesty.

You can also settle this empirically — you own AtomiChron hardware. A
float solution and a fixed one behave very differently after a forced phase
break: reconvergence in tens of minutes versus seconds.

### B5. The three broadcast-UTC specification figures are uncited, and at least
one looks wrong

`how-gnss-time-relates-to-utc.mdx:35`: "40 ns for GPS at 95%, 50 ns for
Galileo, 100 ns for BeiDou." Nothing in `docs/references.md` sources these,
and they are the only uncited numbers in the UTC cluster — which stands out
badly on a page whose argument is *the measured reality is a hundred times
better than the specification.* The size of that ratio depends entirely on
these three numbers.

GPS's 40 ns (UTCOE, 95%, SPS Performance Standard) I am confident in. My
recollection of the other two differs: Galileo's OS-SDD commits to **30 ns**
(95%) for UTC dissemination, and the BDS Open Service Performance Standard's
current figure is tighter than 100 ns — 100 ns reads like the BDS-2-era
number. Check both against the primary documents before publishing, and cite
them in `references.md` like everything else.

### B6. The BIPM calibration uncertainty table does not add up

`can-i-build-my-own-link-to-utc-nist.mdx`, campaign 1001-2022:

| contribution | value |
|---|---|
| antenna cable delay | 0.5 ns |
| position error | 0.28 ns |
| multipath | 0.2 ns |
| counter nonlinearity | 0.1 ns |
| **combined** | **0.91 ns** |

Those four RSS to **0.62 ns**, not 0.91. Either terms are missing (reference
delay and receiver internal delay are the obvious candidates) or the
combination is not RSS. As printed, a careful reader checks it, finds it does
not close, and stops trusting the page — which is a shame, because the
argument it supports (*even BIPM's largest single term is the antenna cable*)
is one of the best in the garden. Add "(largest terms; others omitted)" or
list them all.

### B7. `antenna-position` undersells its own advice

> With reasonable care and a dual-frequency receiver, you can get the time
> error contributed by configured antenna position **below 15 ns**. Into the
> low single digits is a great deal harder.

15 ns is 4.5 m of position; low single digits is about a meter. Both are
*receiver-self-survey* numbers, and the page's own recommendation is to not
do that. An offline PPP or OPUS-Static solution on 24 h of raw observations
lands at centimeters — PePPAR-Fix's operational ARPs carry σ ≈ 12 mm, which
is **40 ps**, three orders of magnitude better than the paragraph implies.
It also contradicts `how-big-is-a-degree`, which calls six decimal places
(≈10 cm, 371 ps) "the practical sweet spot."

Reframe as two regimes, which is what the page actually argues everywhere
else: *survey-in gets you meter-class, so 3–15 ns; an offline solution gets
you centimeter-class, at which point position stops being your limiting term
and the feedline takes over.* That is also the cleanest possible handoff to
the feedline page.

### B8. White Rabbit and IEEE 1588-2019

Two small precision problems in `transferring/distribution.mdx`:

- The table gives White Rabbit's "Standard" as IEEE 1588-2019. WR predates it;
  what happened is that WR's techniques became the **High Accuracy profile** of
  1588-2019. "IEEE 1588-2019 High Accuracy profile" is both shorter and true.
- "IEEE 1588-2019 explicitly accounts for asymmetric links rather than assuming
  they are symmetric" — 1588-2008 already carries a `delayAsymmetry`
  correction. What it does not give you is any way to *measure* it. The
  distinction you want is **a field for asymmetry versus a method for
  determining it**, and the single-fiber/two-wavelength paragraph that follows
  is already the right story.

### B9. GLONASS and Galileo, two clauses each

`how-gnss-time-relates-to-utc.mdx`:

- "GLONASS… follows UTC(SU) and applies leap seconds, so it has no such
  offset." True about leap seconds; GLONASS system time is **UTC(SU) + 3 h**.
  One clause fixes it, and it is the kind of detail that reads as authority.
- The steering list names GPS→UTC(USNO), BeiDou→UTC(NTSC), GLONASS→UTC(SU) and
  omits Galileo — the one constellation the page grades two paragraphs later.
  GST is maintained by the Galileo Time Service Provider against an ensemble of

### B10. Smaller things

- **`limits-of-averaging`**: "the satellites repeat… roughly a sidereal day" is

- **`how-big-is-a-degree`**: row 11 labels 1.11 µm "visible light wavelength."

- **`can-i-build-my-own-link-to-utc-nist`**: two unrelated 16 ns numbers land

## D. Answers to the nine open-question blocks

### D1. `what-makes-an-accurate-timing-receiver` — trait order, smoothing cost,
a seventh trait

**Order.** Dual-band first is right for *accuracy to UTC*, and the measured
NIST jump justifies it. But for the reader you describe in the lede — the one
still at this in two years — **raw observations out** is the trait that
compounds. Everything PePPAR-Fix has been able to do (TDCP, offline surveys,
receiver comparisons, replaying a day against better products months later)
traces to that one capability. Suggest keeping the order and adding a sentence
to trait 4: *"If you expect to still care in two years, this is the one to
weight above its position on this list."* — which you nearly say already.

**Smoothing cost.** Your inference is right and you can state it more strongly.
Hatch smoothing is a one-state recursive filter per satellite per band — a few
flops an epoch. For scale from our own instrumentation: a fully vectorized
multi-constellation PPP filter, which is *vastly* heavier than smoothing, runs
at **0.94% CPU on a Raspberry Pi 4** at 1 Hz. Smoothing is noise against that.

**A seventh trait — yes, and it is a buying trap.** Not the antenna supervisor
(you were right that that is plumbing). It is **whether the firmware actually
reports the quantization error.** You have it as a follow-up under trait 3,
and in practice it is a purchase-level decision, not a follow-up: qErr is a
*timing-firmware* feature. The F9P and the X20P report zero. Same silicon
family, same connectors, and one of them silently hands you a constant instead
of a measurement. That is exactly the shape of trap the garden is good at.

Second candidate, worth a line somewhere: **which band pairs the part actually
supports**, not which bands it lists. The LEA-F9T-11B does L1/L2 *or* L1/L5,
switchable — and NAKs the configuration you did not choose. A datasheet that
lists three bands is not a part that tracks three bands.

### D2. `gnss-time-is-a-prediction` — the correction-stream landscape figure

Still outstanding as a dependency. For a self-consistent publish, either land
it or delete the marker; the page reads complete without it. If it lands
later, the natural caption is the one you sketched (*fresher and lower is
better*), and there is now a result worth putting beside it — see §F3, where
the freshest stream is **not** the steadiest at long tau.

### D3. `benchmarking-clocks` — three-cornered hat, and "replaced by a short"

**Three-cornered hat: we do not use it, and the reason is instructive enough to
put on the page.** 3CH assumes the three units' errors are uncorrelated. Every
comparison in our lab deliberately *correlates* them — shared antenna, shared
reference, shared rack, shared room temperature — because that is how you
isolate the term you are after. So the technique is real, standard, and
structurally unavailable to the setups people actually build. Say that, rather
than cutting it: "needs three, and assumes their errors are uncorrelated, which
is exactly what a shared environment breaks" is already your sentence — it just
deserves to be the point rather than the caveat.

**"Replaced by a short" is the RF-metrology move, not the time-interval one.**
For a time interval counter the standard is a **zero-baseline test**: split one
PPS with a power splitter into both channels and measure. What comes back is
the counter's own single-shot noise *and* its channel-to-channel offset, both
of which you subtract from everything afterwards. We do exactly this (it is why
we have calibration numbers like extint +93.94 ns and TICC −84.31 ns for one
host), and it is reproducible by any reader with a splitter and two equal
cables — which makes it much better copy than "replace the device with a short."

Everything else in the nine holds up.

### D4. `choosing-a-gnss-timescale` — the framing, AtomiChron, TMAS

**The (a)/(b)/(c) framing by *whose datum you land on* is right**, and it is
better than an accuracy ladder because it explains the one thing an accuracy
ladder cannot: why mixing two correction sources destroys AR. PePPAR-Fix has a
whole design document on precisely this (`docs/ac-datum-mixing.md`), whose
thesis is that phase biases from different analysis centers are *calibrations
in different datums, not noise around a common truth* — fine to mix for float
PPP, forbidden for AR. Keep your framing; it is the same one.

**AtomiChron:** see §B4. Cut the compute argument, keep the link budget, and
consider testing it.

**TMAS as a fourth answer: yes, it earns its place here.** It is the only row
that answers "somebody outside my estate has to accept this," and your
decision list in *How to decide, in one pass* would have a hole without it.
The traceability page is the wrong home — traceability is about what makes a
claim acceptable, not about which product to buy.

### D5. `buying-a-clock` — a tenth question, and spec-versus-typical

**Yes, add the regulatory question, and name the regulation.** For a trading
buyer it is **MiFID II RTS 25** — the divergence-from-UTC ceiling (100 µs for
high-frequency algorithmic trading, 1 ms for other in-scope activity) plus a
timestamp-granularity requirement and a documented-traceability requirement.
US readers will be arriving with FINRA/CAT clock-sync obligations instead. See
§E5: this is not just a tenth question, it is arguably the biggest single hole
in the garden's search surface.

The question to ask a vendor is not "are you compliant" — they will say yes.
It is **"what do you produce when the regulator asks me to demonstrate
divergence over a past quarter?"** That is a monitoring-record question, it is
the one thing on your list that a box either does or does not do, and it
connects straight to the traceability page.

**Spec versus typical versus distribution holds.** Clock vendors publish
"typical" almost universally and distributions almost never. The instinct
transfers intact; if anything this market is worse than most, because a single
"±15 ns" with no interval, no reference and no population attached is
considered a complete specification.

### D6. `designing-a-clock` — observe/actuate, a missing category, whether the
page should exist

**Question 4 generalizes — keep it, and it is the strongest item on the page.**
It is not specific to that plant. The structure is: every actuation injects the
actuator's quantization noise, falling as σ_q/√τ; every interval of coasting
accumulates the oscillator's free-running wander, growing as σ_DO(1 s)·√τ. Two
curves, one minimum, and the minimum is not at either end. The deciding
parameter is actuator resolution, which moves the answer six orders of
magnitude:

| plant | σ_q | σ_DO(1 s) | best actuation interval |
|---|---|---|---|
| OCXO + 16-bit DAC | ≈18 ps | ≈18 ps | ≈1 s — the knee is in the operating range |
| TCXO + PHC `adjfine` | ≈15 fs | ≈1.17 ns | ≈13 µs — coasting never wins, fire as fast as data arrives |

Same control problem, opposite conclusion. And the empirical payoff you have
already lived: on the lab OCXO, correcting at 1 Hz was **worse than coasting**.

**The missing category is not temperature — it is the actuator.** "What are you
steering *with*, how finely, over what range, and is the range symmetric?" is
absent from the page and it decides more than most of the nine. The failure
modes are all things we have actually hit: no measured actuator gain (never
inherit one from a datasheet — measure it or refuse to run); a DAC code range
that saturates the EFC; an OCXO whose pull range is *asymmetric*, so the
slow side runs out of authority before the fast side; and the general trap of
assuming a "center code" exists rather than anchoring to a measured
code-minimum and slope.

**Temperature deserves a question too**, and you have better stories for it
than most people writing about clocks: an OCXO that moved 4 ppb from a ceiling
fan, an oven that got *worse* when over-insulated because the loop needs heat
loss to regulate against, and a back-yard unit that entered a wind-driven limit
cycle by day and recovered every night.

**Should the page exist? Yes.** It is the terminus of the questions arc, and
"which of my errors are compensable" is the one framing on the whole site that
a designer cannot get anywhere else. It is also the page that earns the
garden's credibility with the small readership that will link to you.

### D7. `agreement-is-not-accuracy` — four uses of "common-mode"

Keep the term. It is load-bearing, it is the correct word, it is glossed and
linked, and three other pages depend on the reader having met it. The
adjustment worth making is one clause on **first** use — *"common-mode: an
error that lands on every clock in the ensemble equally"* — after which the
term can carry the rest of the page unaided. That is your own house rule
("gloss on first use, link the glossary, never make the jargon a precondition
for arriving"), applied.

### D8. `comparing-two-distant-clocks` — the framing, and where fairness goes

**The "third clock, and removing its error" framing works**, and it is not
imposed. It is the actual structure of the field: the residual equation you
print makes it a *derivation* rather than a device, and the payoff — common
view removes the satellite's error by differencing two stations that *saw* it,
PPP transfer removes the stream's datum by differencing two stations that
*used* it — is genuinely the same move one level up. That callout is the best
paragraph on the page. Keep all of it.

**Move the fairness up, but only one sentence of it.** For a reader who
remembers the original trumpeting, a full section at the end reads as a
concession. Put one line immediately after the "why has BIPM stopped using it"
heading — *"none of what follows makes it a bad technique, and there is a
section at the end saying so"* — and leave the section where it is. That way
the reader is not braced against you while reading the evidence, and the
detailed defense still lands after they have the numbers.

### D9. `can-i-build-my-own-link-to-utc-nist` — frequency framing, amateur
calibration, your own numbers

**Yes, lead with the frequency argument.** It is the strongest true claim on
the page, and the site already owns the machinery to make it in one line: an
unknown constant delay is an *offset* error, not a *frequency* error, and your
`elapsed-time` page has already taught the reader that those are two
independent ways for a clock to be wrong and that offsets cancel in
differences. Leading with it means the page's answer is a *result* — "a superb
frequency reference, free" — rather than a disappointment.

**There is an amateur calibration path, and you have already described it.**
See §C1. Píriz's co-located-calibrated-receiver method is exactly that path;
so is borrowing time on a calibrated unit, so is differencing against a
neighbouring lab, and so is truth-transfer from a calibrated appliance you own
(which is what we do for timestamper arms). What you inherit is that chain's
own floor — 1–2 ns by Píriz's own statement, which the page already quotes.
Do not close the door; state the price of walking through it.

**Yes, the page wants your numbers, and it already has the best of them.** The
NIST-observations sanity check and the [UTC(USNO) − UTC(NIST)] result are the
two things nobody else's page has, and the frequency/time split falls out of
the second one in a single table. The follow-up that would make it a
different page is the one the page itself names: **redo it on an externally
clocked receiver** — a geodetic module taking your OCXO on its reference
input, so the receiver's clock estimate *is* your oscillator's error against
GNSS with no second hop. You own hardware that can do this. That is the
experiment, and it is a page.

## E. Omissions that block self-consistency

Ranked by how loudly the existing pages promise them.

### E1. Holdover has no Home (highest priority)

There is a glossary entry, and holdover carries real weight on five pages: a
whole vendor question (#5), a whole design question (#8), the "which is why the
local oscillator does the real work" callout, and the free-path value list.
Every one of them links to the glossary, which is two sentences.

By the garden's own H/S/M rule this is a concept with Summaries and Mentions
and no Home. The reader's question is **"what happens when I lose GPS?"** —
which is one of the two or three most-searched questions in datacenter timing,
and one you have measured directly. Minimum viable page: what holdover is and
why it is not free-running; why a holdover figure is meaningless without a
threshold and an oscillator; that the dominant term is usually thermal, not the
oscillator's intrinsic noise; and how to measure your own.

### E2. Oscillators have no Home

Four pages carry `tags: ["oscillators"]`, and "a good oscillator" is
load-bearing in five, but no page ever says what one is or how to choose.
Reader question: **"do I need an OCXO, or will a TCXO do?"** You can answer it
with a number: at ADEV(1 s) ≈ 1e-10 a TCXO's integrated free-running noise
above a 1 Hz loop bandwidth consumes an entire nanosecond-class budget by
itself, and no amount of servo tuning recovers it; ~1e-11 is the entry ticket.
That is a sharper answer than anything a vendor page will give.

### E3. PPS has no glossary entry

See §A3 — mechanical, five minutes, and it fixes the broken link.

### E4. "Silently wrong" is promised on four pages and never answered

The phrase *the silently wrong theme running through this site* appears
explicitly; the antenna page's whole argument is that a moved antenna raises no
alarm; the traceability page requires "a monitoring record showing the system
stayed within budget over the period being claimed." Nothing tells the reader
how to notice. A short **"how do you know it is still right?"** page would
close a loop the garden opens repeatedly — two independent sources compared
against each other and something that complains when they diverge, which is
already argued on the acquiring page and needs only to be given a home.

If it will not fit before the deadline, the honest alternative is to phrase the
"silently wrong" claims as observations rather than as promises of a later
answer.

## F. Future topics, ranked by how much of it only you have

1. **The Goldilocks actuation interval.** Fully drafted in your plan doc, the
   figure exists, the two-plant table is above in §D6. It generalizes past
   GPSDOs to any control loop with a noisy actuator, which makes it the rare
   garden page that a non-timing reader would link to. Strongest unbuilt page
   you have.

2. **Known-good observations — grading your own chain.** Run your pipeline on
   observations from a station whose clock *is* UTC(k) (PTBB, BRUX,
   NIST00USA). Any wander in the clock bias your filter computes cannot be the
   clock, so it is a fingerprint of everything else — corrections, models,
   multipath, your own bugs. The technique came to us from Ole Petter
   Rønningen and deserves the credit line. `can-i-build-my-own-link` already
   does half of it as a sanity check without naming it as a method; it is a
   method, it is free, and any reader can reproduce it.

3. **Fresher is not automatically steadier.** From the PTBB/BRUX work: at
   *both* stations, plain broadcast ephemeris was **steadier at long tau** than
   the real-time IGS combined SSR stream, whose own clock datum humped about
   15 ns over three hours and came back. Both floor at ~50 ps TDEV(1 s), so
   this is not a short-tau story. That directly qualifies
   `gnss-time-is-a-prediction`'s "the fastest and most accurate of the three,"
   and it is the sort of counterintuitive measured result that makes a page the
   11th rather than the 1001st.

4. **The expensive thermometer.** Sawtooth quantization, qErr, and the story
   you already have — the sawtooth pattern that reversed when the air
   conditioning came on. Pairs with the seventh receiver trait in §D1, and it
   is the most vivid teaching device in your material after the archer.

5. **Per-unit variance beats per-model difference.** Measured across our
   fleet: the spread between two nominally identical F9Ts was *larger* than the
   gap between an F9T and an F9P. It undermines the entire "which receiver
   should I buy" genre, and it is the kind of finding that only falls out of
   having several of the same box on one antenna.

6. **Actuator resolution and control authority.** §D6's missing category, as a
   page. Nobody writes this down: measure your gain or refuse to run, no magic
   center code, code-symmetry versus pull-symmetry, asymmetric pull range,
   EFC saturation.

7. **Datums, reference frames, and the ground moving.** Your plan already has
   the Barrow station and the Italian earthquake, and PePPAR-Fix has the
   NAD83↔ITRF machinery and the solid-tide arithmetic. "Why does my antenna
   move 15 cm in seven years, and does it matter?" — 500 ps says yes.

8. **Spoofing, jamming, and OSNMA.** Already gestured at on the acquiring page
