Handing time *out* across your own estate is one problem, and the
[protocols page](/timekeeping/transferring/distribution/) is about that one:
you own both ends, you own the wire, and one end is unambiguously the source.

This is the other problem. Two clocks, a continent apart, owned by different
people, and **neither one is the source for the other**. You want to know how
far apart they are. There is no cable, and there is not going to be one.

If the first problem is a LAN, this is the WAN — and as with networking, the
techniques that work over the long haul look almost nothing like the ones that
work in a building.

## What stands in the way is the satellite's own clock

Every technique here uses the same instrument: a GNSS receiver watching
satellites, timestamping their signals against the local clock. For your
receiver **r** watching satellite **s**, what you record is

> arrival time = geometry + (your clock error − **the satellite's clock error**)
> + atmosphere + noise

You want your clock error. The geometry you know, if you surveyed properly. The
atmosphere you can measure or model. But
[the satellite's clock is drifting and nobody is steering it](/timekeeping/utc/gnss-time-is-a-prediction/),
and its error is far larger than the thing you are trying to measure.

**Every technique on this page is a different way of disposing of that one
term.** Once you see that, the whole field collapses into three options.

## Common view: cancel it with a second measurement

Have a second station watch **the same satellite at the same instant**. It
records the same satellite clock error you did. Subtract the two records and
that term **cancels exactly** — not modelled, not estimated, gone. What is left
is the difference between the two local clocks.

It is a beautiful idea, and its beauty is that it needs nothing else. No
correction product, no internet, no subscription, no analysis center. Two
receivers, a shared satellite, and arithmetic. That is why it could be built in
the 1980s, and why it carried international timekeeping for two decades.

And if the far end's clock is a national laboratory's realization of UTC, the
answer lands directly on **UTC(NIST)** or UTC(USNO) — a *named* timescale that
somebody publishes, which turns out to be the thing that matters most.

## Then why has BIPM stopped using it?

Because the error it was invented to cancel no longer exists.

BIPM's own 2006 working document to the CCTF puts it on one axis, and this is
the single most clarifying thing I found while writing this page:

| era | error in the GPS signal | technique |
|---|---|---|
| 1991 | **~30 ns** — "GPS Time S.A. on" | common view |
| 2000 | ~6 ns — "GPS Time S.A. off" | |
| 2005 | **~1 ns** — "IGS Time" | all-in-view |

**Selective Availability** was the deliberate dither the US applied to GPS
satellite clocks, worth tens of nanoseconds. Common view cancelled it perfectly,
which made common view worth a great deal. SA was switched off in May 2000.
Precise orbit and clock products then took the residual to about a nanosecond —
and at that point *what common view cancels is worth less than what it costs.*

### What it costs is the part people skip

Requiring the same satellite at the same instant is not free:

> Disadvantages (**especially long baselines**): Observed satellite number
> reduced; Low satellites, low S/N ratio; Multi-Bridge System of TAI Network.

You throw away every satellite the far end cannot see, and you are pushed toward
the low-elevation ones you can both barely reach — which are exactly the
satellites with the worst signal-to-noise and the most atmosphere in the way.
The longer the [baseline](/timekeeping/glossary/#baseline), the worse the deal.
BIPM measured it:

| baseline | length | gain from dropping common view |
|---|---|---|
| OP–PTB | 600 km | ~1–4 % |
| NPL–PTB | 700 km | ~0 % |
| NPL–USNO | 6500 km | ~10–20 % |
| **NIST–PTB** | **10 000 km** | **~28–35 %** |

Nothing at 600 km, a third at 10,000. Which is the same sentence as *common view
only cancels what both ends genuinely share*, written in numbers.

## All-in-view, and then PPP

**All-in-view** drops the simultaneity requirement. Each station uses *every*
satellite it can see, solves its own offset against the constellation timescale
using a precise product, and only then are the two results differenced. BIPM
switched TAI links to it in **September 2006**, noting that it delivered
"improved TAI time transfer stability **without any new investment** — no
hardware updating required."

**PPP time transfer** goes one better by adding the carrier phase. Each station
runs its own [PPP](/timekeeping/glossary/#ppp) solution against the *same*
product, and the two solutions are differenced. The product's datum appears in
both and cancels — exactly the way the satellite clock cancelled in common view,
but now with a far quieter observable and no requirement that the two stations
ever look at the same satellite. BIPM has used it for UTC since **2009**.

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="The trick is the same trick, applied one level up">
	Common view cancels the <em>satellite's clock</em> by differencing two
	stations that both saw it. PPP time transfer cancels the <em>product's
	datum</em> by differencing two stations that both used it.

	Same move. The second version just picks a quantity that is easier to share —
	because two stations can always use the same correction product, and cannot
	always see the same satellite.
</CalloutBox>

## The receipts, from a file we download every month

*Circular T* Section 5 lists the technique for every link used to compute TAI.
For issue 462, June 2026:

| technique | links | link instability (uStb) |
|---|---|---|
| **GPSPPP** | **48** | 0.3 ns |
| GPS P3 — all-in-view, dual-frequency code | 16 | 0.7 ns |
| GPS MC — all-in-view, single-frequency code | 10 | 1.5–3.0 ns |
| TWGPPP — two-way combined with GPS PPP | 7 | 0.3 ns |
| TWSDRR, TWSTFT — two-way | 3 | 0.3–0.5 ns |
| no data this period | 2 | — |

**Not one common-view link, out of 86.** And that is not a quirk of naming: the
*Explanatory supplement to Circular T* defines the codes, and exactly one of
them is common view —

> **GLN MC** for GLONASS **common-view** multi-channel C/A data

— a GLONASS technique that no link used. Every GPS code in BIPM's current
vocabulary is all-in-view or PPP.

<CalloutBox type="warn" title="Including NIST's own link">
	<code>NIST/PTB &nbsp; TWGPPP &nbsp; NIST01/PTB05 &nbsp; 0593-2024 &nbsp;
	0.3 &nbsp; 2.1 &nbsp; 0.5</code>

	NIST's link into international atomic time is two-way satellite time transfer
	combined with <strong>GPS PPP</strong>. The organization that made
	common view famous does not use it to connect its own timescale to the world.
</CalloutBox>

Read the uncertainty column and the ladder is plain: **PPP links are 0.3 ns
stable, dual-frequency code links 0.7 ns, single-frequency code links 1.5 to
3 ns.** A factor of two to ten, for the same antennas on the same roofs.

## So what is your answer referenced to?

This is the part that took me longest to see, and it is the bottom band of the
figure.

A [PPP solution](/timekeeping/utc/gnss-time-is-a-prediction/) of the ordinary
kind — one station, one product, solve for your clock — gives you an excellent
number referenced to **the analysis center's datum**. That datum is stable and
perfectly serviceable. It is also unpublished, uncertified, and appears in no
document that anybody outside that analysis center is obliged to accept. Your
chain stops there.

Difference two PPP solutions and the datum cancels. What you are left with is
your clock against **UTC(NIST)** — which appears in *Circular T* Section 1 every
month, with a published [UTC − UTC(NIST)] and a stated uncertainty. Your chain
does not stop; it continues, through NIST, to UTC itself.

So the anchor is not tighter in the sense of quieter. It is tighter in the sense
that **it connects to something**. You trade a private zero for a public one,
and [traceability](/timekeeping/utc/traceability/) is precisely the business of
public zeros.

## Being fair to common view

None of the above makes it a bad technique, and a page that implied so would be
selling something.

- **The cancellation is exact.** No model, no product, no assumption. There is
  an honesty to that which the alternatives do not have.
- **It needs nothing you have to subscribe to.** In an outage, in a lab with no
  internet, on hardware from 1995, it still works.
- **It is thoroughly standardized.** CGGTTS is a stable, documented format with
  decades of continuity behind it, and continuity is worth real money in
  metrology.
- **Short baselines lose almost nothing.** BIPM measured ~0 % gain at 700 km.
  If your reference is regional rather than continental, the case against is
  much weaker.
- **And what a service like TMAS sells is not the technique anyway.** It sells a
  calibrated end, a ten-minute feed, and a report with NIST's uncertainty on it.
  Those would still be worth buying wrapped around any technique.

## If somebody is selling you common view

Three questions, in the spirit of the
[vendor questions](/timekeeping/questions/buying-a-clock/) page:

1. **"What is my baseline to your reference, and what does the technique cost me
   at that distance?"** Under a thousand kilometers, very little. Across an
   ocean, ask for numbers.
2. **"Single-frequency or dual?"** The deployed fleet matters more than the
   technique. An L1-only receiver models the ionosphere rather than measuring
   it, and over long baselines that is the dominant term.
3. **"Is this what BIPM uses for your own link into TAI?"** For NIST the answer
   is on the public record and it is no. That is not a gotcha — it is a way of
   asking what the technique is actually being chosen *for*, which is usually
   continuity, calibration, and a report rather than performance.
