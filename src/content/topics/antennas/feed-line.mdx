Signals take time to travel down the antenna cable, and nothing in your system
corrects for it. That delay lands directly in your offset from UTC, and in a
typical datacenter installation it is larger than the errors people spend their
attention on.

This is not usually anybody's mistake. It is a reasonable assumption that has
stopped being reasonable.

## Why it goes wrong even when everyone is competent

You may not have run the feed line. You may not own it. In a datacenter, quite
possibly somebody ran it years ago on a work order, and did a perfectly good job
by every standard that applied to them.

And here is the thing they would have had no reason to think about: **for
almost every cable in the building, length does not matter.** Ethernet, fiber,
power — you run what you need, within limits, and nothing downstream cares
whether it was 12 meters or 30. That instinct is correct nearly everywhere. It
happens to be wrong for exactly one cable in the room.

So the number is often unrecorded, because nobody recording it knew it was a
number worth recording.

## What actually matters is the delay, not the length

Worth being precise here, because the distinction turns out to be the whole
practical problem.

The quantity your clock needs is the **delay** — how long the signal takes to
get from the antenna to the receiver. Length is not that quantity. Length is one
input to calculating it, and the other input is the
[velocity factor](/timekeeping/glossary/#velocity-factor) of the specific coax:
the fraction of the speed of light at which a signal moves down it.

That fraction varies more than you might expect. Solid-polyethylene coax runs
about 0.66; foam dielectric about 0.85. In delay terms:

| Coax dielectric | Velocity factor | Delay |
|---|---|---|
| Solid polyethylene | 0.66 | 5.05 ns/m |
| Foam | 0.85 | 3.92 ns/m |

Same length, and nearly 30% different delay.

Look at the ratio in both screenshots: 10 m ↔ 50 ns, 20 m ↔ 100 ns. **Five
nanoseconds per meter, fixed.** That is a velocity factor of 0.66 baked in — a
perfectly sensible default, and the correct one for solid-polyethylene coax.

If your cable is foam dielectric, its real delay is 3.92 ns/m. Configure 20 m
"by length" and the clock applies 101 ns of compensation to a cable that
actually delays by 78 ns. **You have just introduced 23 ns of error while
carefully entering a correct number.**

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Configure by delay when you are offered the choice">
	Entering a length asks the equipment to guess your velocity factor. Entering a
	delay does not — it is the quantity that matters, stated directly, with no
	assumption in the middle.
</CalloutBox>

## Getting the number

Two things to find out, and neither needs test equipment.

**The velocity factor** comes from the coax manufacturer. Identify the cable
type — it is usually printed on the jacket along with everything else — and look
up its published velocity factor. This is a datasheet lookup, not a measurement.

**The length**, if you must derive delay from it, is often printed on the jacket
too. Most coax carries sequential length markers every foot or meter. If the run
is already installed, you can frequently read the marker at each end and
subtract, without pulling anything or owning a TDR. The cable has been telling
you its length the whole time.

Add a little for the service loop you cannot see, be honest that it is an
estimate, and remember that the estimate is being multiplied by a velocity
factor you should also have looked up.

## What this is worth

A 30 m run misjudged by one velocity factor is about 34 ns. A run whose length
is wrong by 3 m is about 15 ns. Both are large compared with the survey errors
that occupy far more attention, and both are fixed by reading two labels and
typing one number.

It ends in the same place as
[not letting the receiver survey itself](/timekeeping/antennas/receiver-self-survey/):
measure it, or look it up, and configure the result. Do not accept a default and
do not guess.
