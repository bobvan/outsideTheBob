A [timescale](/timekeeping/datacenters/timescales/) needs a unit, an origin, and
a count. Measuring a *duration* needs only the first and the last.

You do not need to know what time it is to know how long something took. You need
a tick of known length and something to count ticks with. No origin, no agreement
with anybody else, no reference to UTC.

That makes elapsed time a fundamentally cheaper problem, and it is worth knowing
when you have that problem rather than the expensive one.

## How much cheaper

Consider a trader measuring the interval between a market data tick arriving and
an order going out — say 2.5 µs.

The only thing that can corrupt that measurement is the counter running at the
wrong rate. Suppose it is off by 1 part per million, which is a poor oscillator
by timing standards:

> 2.5 µs × 1 ppm = **2.5 picoseconds** of error.

Two and a half picoseconds. Under a hundredth of a millimeter in
[distance terms](/timekeeping/datacenters/how-big-is-a-nanosecond/). A mediocre
oscillator measures a microsecond-scale interval essentially perfectly, because
frequency error scales with the interval — and the interval is short.

Meanwhile, that same clock could be a full millisecond away from UTC and this
measurement would not notice, because the offset appears at both ends of the
subtraction and cancels.

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Short intervals forgive bad clocks">
	Frequency error contributes in proportion to the duration measured. Over
	microseconds, almost any oscillator is fine. The expensive requirements come
	from needing to agree with somebody else about <em>when</em> — not from needing
	to know <em>how long</em>.
</CalloutBox>

## Where it stops being true

The forgiveness above has limits, and they are worth stating because that is
where the reasoning breaks:

**Long intervals.** The same 1 ppm that costs 2.5 ps over 2.5 µs costs 86 ms
over a day. Frequency error scales with duration, so an interval long enough
eventually needs a good oscillator after all.

**Intervals measured by two different clocks.** The cancellation above depends on
both timestamps coming from the same counter. Start on one clock and stop on
another and you are no longer measuring elapsed time — you are measuring elapsed
time *plus* the offset between two clocks, which is the
[expensive problem](/timekeeping/two-clock/agreement-is-not-accuracy/) in
disguise.

**Anything that must be compared with somebody else's record.** A duration you
report to a regulator or a counterparty has to be anchored to a shared timescale
to mean anything to them, at which point you need the origin after all.

## Which is the useful question

Before buying accuracy, work out which of these you actually have:

- **How long did it take?** — one clock, short interval, cheap.
- **In what order did these happen?** — several clocks that must agree with each
  other, moderately expensive.
- **When exactly did this happen?** — agreement with UTC, most expensive.

Organizations routinely buy the third when they needed the first. The
[questions to ask a vendor](/timekeeping/questions/buying-a-clock/) start here for
exactly that reason.
